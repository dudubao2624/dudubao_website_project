Custom Color Magnetic Tiles: Planning a Color Change

Planning custom color magnetic tiles starts with a reference set and a written map of what will change. Identify the components, color references, sample purpose and packaging updates together. A palette name is a starting idea; your buying decision needs an approved physical reference and a record that connects it to the final contents, box and instructions.

Custom color magnetic tiles: define the change before sampling

Choose the existing configuration closest to your product plan. For a tile-building direction, compare D01-C, with 102 pieces, and D10-C, with 219 pieces. Counts include listed accessories; the booklet is listed separately. Confirm the selected sample and corresponding packaging before using either configuration as your project reference.

Write a change list with three categories: colors to change, components to retain and questions needing feasibility review. Keep changes to shapes, quantities or functions on separate lines. This guide focuses on a color change to a selected set; a new structure or component needs its own project review.

Give every proposed colorway an item reference, intended quantity and packaging language. If two palettes share the same component mix, record that relationship explicitly. A shared base set should not make the samples, artwork or approval decisions indistinguishable.

Step 1: choose color references that both teams can identify

For each proposed color, provide a named reference system and code, such as a Pantone reference where appropriate, together with the relevant guide or edition. If a physical object or swatch is the preferred reference, label it and record which face or area represents the target. Agree which reference the project team will use before preparing the sample.

Use descriptive names such as soft blue only as labels beside the reference. Identify whether the reference applies to the component body, a printed detail or the box artwork. Treat a proposed color code as an input for review, then confirm the appearance on the actual sampled component. Do not assume the code itself approves the finished result.

Decide how disagreements between references will be handled. If a brand file, swatch and concept image show different directions, choose the controlling reference before sampling. Keep the other images marked as explanatory material so they cannot become competing approval standards.

Step 2: map each color to a component and quantity

Prepare a shape-and-color list for the whole proposed set. For each line, record the component, quantity, existing reference, proposed color and whether it changes. Reconcile the quantities with the complete component list. The set configuration guide helps organize that count.

Do not silently extend a tile color decision to every accessory. For a track-based project, C01-C has 99 pieces, including one LED track car. Its track pieces and LED track car are ABS. Identify which of those parts your color proposal concerns and confirm feasibility; other components need their own entries and material review.

For example, if your brief changes the square tiles but retains the accessories, mark the retained parts as well as the changed ones. When the sample arrives, compare it against both groups. This makes an unintended change visible without relying on memory.

Step 3: plan the custom color sample and its purpose

State the question the sample must answer: selection between palettes, appearance on particular shapes or review of the full proposed set. Agree which components and packaging elements it will represent. If a sample shows only part of the proposal, list the remaining items as open rather than approving the complete product.

In-stock samples take about 7 days; custom color or packaging samples take about 15 days. These are preparation times, excluding transit. Sample arrangements and schedule are confirmed per project. Keep your review period and any revision round as separate entries; about 15 days is not the entire development or delivery cycle.

Prepare your references and consolidated change list for the sample discussion. Agree what the team needs before preparation starts and which questions require feasibility confirmation. Use the MOQ and samples guide to record the sample scope. Avoid starting parallel sample requests from different artwork or palette revisions.

Step 4: review physical colors and record a clear decision

Compare the labelled physical sample with the agreed reference. Use the same review setup for each proposed version and note the lighting and background used. Examine the changed shapes individually, then review the assembled palette beside the retained components. Keep photographs with the record to explain comments and identify the sample.

Agree the acceptance criteria and who makes the final decision for the project. If a measurable color criterion is needed, have the relevant teams agree the method and limits before using it. Do not invent a tolerance or assume that a screen preview establishes the accepted physical color.

Write comments by component and sample reference. Replace “make it warmer” with the affected part, controlling swatch and the difference to review. After revision, name the new sample and record whether it replaces the earlier one. Retain the approved physical reference and confirmation record for the agreed project purpose.

Step 5: coordinate the palette with packaging and instructions

Keep product-color approval and print-color approval as separate decisions. Identify the intended printed references for the box and booklet, then review them with the selected packaging sample. Your product palette and brand artwork should have an agreed relationship, with each approval tied to the material actually reviewed.

List every image or instruction affected by the change: front-panel build, contents diagram, shape key, booklet steps and proposed listing photographs. If an instruction identifies parts by color, check those names against the new palette. Update the affected artwork after the product reference is settled, then cross-check it with the selected contents.

Use the packaging and artwork guide for the confirmed dieline and file handover. A color change does not establish a different package feature: D10-C has no carry handle. If the packaging format is also changing, record that proposal separately and review the resulting sample.

Step 6: confirm one colorway, sample and order version

Bring the component list, approved physical color reference, sample identifier, box artwork and booklet revision into one confirmation record. Add quantity per colorway, target market and the person responsible for each decision. Mark remaining conditions clearly before treating the proposed version as complete.

Custom and private-label orders start at 500 sets per SKU, subject to project confirmation. List every proposed palette and quantity separately; an assortment total should not be treated as confirmation for all versions. Production takes 30–40 days after the sample and order details are confirmed. Place that starting point after the relevant approvals in your plan.

We have EN 71 reports for selected samples; coverage of the current sets and target-market documentation is confirmed per project.

Include the color change in the project document review. Keep its component and sample references beside the supporting file so the responsible reviewer can identify what changed. The first-order document checklist helps assign the remaining questions and review points.

Common color-change pitfalls to catch early

  • A palette name without a controlling reference. Identify the code or physical swatch and the component it concerns.
  • One approved part standing for the whole set. Record which shapes, accessories and packaging the sample represents.
  • Changed colors with unchanged instructions. Review color names, diagrams and build photographs together.
  • A box preview used as product-color approval. Keep the physical component and printed packaging decisions distinct.
  • An unrecorded change after approval. Identify the affected sample and artwork references, then reopen the relevant review.

The custom color magnetic tiles decision checklist

  • One base SKU and complete component list are identified.
  • Each changed and retained component has a clear color entry.
  • The controlling references and physical sample scope are agreed.
  • Sample comments have a reviewer, decision and current reference.
  • Box, booklet and product images represent the selected palette.
  • Quantity per version, document questions and order details are recorded.
  • The schedule separates preparation, transit, review and production.

Buyer questions

Is a color reference code enough to approve custom color magnetic tiles?

Use a named reference system and code, or a labelled physical swatch, to define the target. Confirm appearance on the actual sampled components and retain the agreed physical reference. Record which parts and version the approval covers.

How long does a custom color sample take?

Custom color or packaging samples take about 15 days. These are preparation times, excluding transit. Confirm scope and schedule per project, and allow separately for your review and any revision round.

Does approving a product color also approve the box?

Keep product-color and print-color approvals separate. Review the selected components and packaging sample, then align box images, contents diagrams and instructions with the approved palette and configuration.
