Private Label Magnetic Tiles: A Buyer's Checklist

Private label magnetic tiles need a coordinated brief: the selected set, your brand files, the packaging layout and the information that belongs on it. Before a designer finishes the box, make sure the logo, instruction languages, barcode and warning locations have an owner. Use this checklist to prepare those decisions, then see our private-label development page for the service and sample-review pathway.

Private label magnetic tiles start with a product reference

Give each proposed item a model, version and component list before assembling the brand files. For a tile-building range, compare D01-C, with 102 pieces, with D10-C, with 219 pieces. If your concept uses sound or a route, review A03-T, the 73-piece bell ball run, or C01-C, the 99-piece track set with an LED track car.

These are configuration references. Counts include listed accessories, with the booklet listed separately; confirm the selected sample and corresponding packaging. Record proposed changes beside the reference instead of editing the contents informally as artwork develops. The box image, contents panel and instructions should all describe the same version.

1. Prepare a usable logo and placement brief

Give your designer an editable vector logo and a preview showing the intended appearance. Identify the approved brand name, color references and logo variants. Specify where each version should appear: the box, booklet, insert or proposed tile artwork. Confirm the required production files for the selected project.

A logo file alone does not explain placement. Mark the intended panel and orientation, and identify any clear-space preferences your designer must preserve. If direct printing on tiles is proposed, identify the component, side, position and size for review. Keep packaging approval and proposed tile printing as separate checklist items so one cannot be mistaken for the other.

Ready when: the project folder contains the editable logo, approved preview, color references and a placement list, all tied to the same brand-file revision.

2. Write a packaging brief before final layout

Describe the packaging format you want reviewed, the product it must contain and the presentation you want the box to show. Use a project-confirmed dieline and dimensions for the selected packaging. The packaging and artwork guide explains how to organize files around that template.

List the product name, contents description, proposed build images, language panels, barcode area and warning-text areas. Check that any illustrated build uses the agreed contents, or identify additional pieces clearly during review. A photo from a different configuration should not become the box's contents promise.

Mark folds, cuts and panel orientation on the layout review. Ask your designer and the project team to check the artwork on the confirmed template before preparing the packaging sample. A flat preview helps review copy; the packaging sample helps review how the panels and product presentation work together.

Ready when: the packaging format, dieline revision, contents reference and proposed panel layout are identified in one brief.

3. Plan instruction languages and review responsibilities

List every required instruction language rather than writing only “multilingual booklet.” Assign someone to supply or approve each translation, and identify the source text they should use. Keep the product name and component names consistent across the box, booklet and contents list.

For a set with an LED track car or other functional accessories, have the instructions reviewed against the selected sample. Confirm the operating details and any relevant setup information for that exact version. Do not infer the power source or included items from the appearance of a car in an older image.

Check that diagrams use the selected components and that the sequence is understandable alongside the approved text. If the component mix changes, flag the affected illustrations and translations for review. Treat the instruction revision as part of the product specification.

Ready when: the language list, source text, translated files, diagram references and final reviewer are recorded.

4. Map the barcode to the exact branded item

Provide the barcode information for each intended branded SKU and confirm who approves it. Keep a simple mapping between your item reference, supplier model, version, packaging language and barcode artwork. If two versions need different identifiers under your own product setup, make that distinction clear before layout.

Do not copy a code from a reference box or an older artwork file merely because the model looks similar. Have the brand's responsible person confirm the intended identifier and its relationship to the exact packaged item. The barcode on a sample image is a reference to check, not an automatic assignment to your private-label product.

Ask the packaging reviewer to check the barcode area on the confirmed dieline, including its orientation and surrounding artwork. Include a readability check on the final packaging sample in the project review plan.

Ready when: each item has an approved barcode file, a confirmed mapping and a marked position on its packaging layout.

5. Reserve warning locations and confirm the text

Identify the intended market and language requirements early enough to reserve space for the relevant information. Have the responsible reviewer confirm the warning wording, location and presentation for the selected configuration. Use the box and booklet layouts to show exactly where approved text belongs; do not leave this as an unnamed final design task.

Review proposed age grading and other product statements against the selected configuration and market requirements. An age statement or symbol from a reference design should not be adopted automatically. Keep the approved wording with the artwork revision so it can be checked again after layout changes.

We have EN 71 reports for selected samples; coverage of the current sets and target-market documentation is confirmed per project. Complete that project-specific review alongside the artwork. A report reference or a packaging example does not by itself settle the text for a changed private-label set.

Ready when: the reviewer, approved wording, languages and locations on the box or instructions are recorded, with any open items clearly assigned.

6. Check the complete file handover before sampling

Use one folder and a short index so the project team can find the intended files. Record a revision and approval status for each item below. Avoid a mixture of final artwork and superseded previews with similar names.

Common private-label mistakes to catch before approval

  • Several logo versions in circulation: identify one approved revision and mark its placements.
  • A contents panel copied from another set: compare each named part and image with the selected list.
  • Languages added after layout: establish the language list and text responsibilities before final design.
  • A barcode inherited from a reference file: confirm the exact branded item and approved identifier.
  • Warning text left without a location: mark its panel or instruction position for review.
  • Approval sent as “looks good”: name the artwork, sample and remaining conditions in the confirmation record.

Before approving, open the final box file and booklet beside the selected contents list. Check product names, images, identifiers and revisions together. This final cross-check can reveal mismatches that are easy to miss when each file is reviewed separately.

Buyer questions

What should a brand prepare first?

Start with the reference SKU and proposed changes, then gather the editable logo, packaging brief, instruction language list, barcode information and warning-text review responsibilities. Mark anything undecided so the project team can confirm the next step.

Can I reuse artwork from a reference box?

Use it to explain a direction, then review the design against your selected configuration, brand files and confirmed dieline. Contents, images, barcode information and warning text need confirmation for the exact private-label version.

What should the final approval identify?

Identify the selected sample, component list, color references, packaging and booklet revisions, barcode mapping and approved text locations. Record unresolved items and agree whether a change needs another sample or artwork review.
